Our Research

The packaging of eukaryotic DNA into chromatin allows differential compartmentalization of the same genome into transcriptionally active (euchromatic) and heritably repressed (heterochromatic) states. Such compartmentalization enables specific transcriptional programs for stabilizing cell identity. Defects in chromatin regulation can cause cells to have an "identity crisis", leading to diseases like cancer. However much remains unknown about how specific chromatin states are formed and regulated. To make some of this unknown known we study the intrinsic biochemical and biophysical behaviors of chromatin and chromatin regulators from the molecular scale to the mesoscale. Our findings allow us to imagine and test how these behaviors regulate genome function in different cellular contexts. While our approaches are rooted in biochemical and biophysical thinking, we constantly seek out new methods based on where the questions lead us. To date our work has benefited from methods in mechanistic enzymology, single-molecule biochemistry, structural biology, genetics and cell biology. Our questions and findings are summarized below.

How are chromatin dynamics regulated by ATP-dependent and independent mechanisms?

+

Within cells, chromatin states are not statically fixed but rather are dynamically maintained by multiple factors that act on the smallest unit of chromatin, a nucleosome. A nucleosome contains ~147 bp of DNA wrapped around an octamer of histone proteins. Central regulators of nucleosome mobilization are ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling enzymes, which use ATP to catalyze nucleosome dynamics. These conserved enzymes fall into four main classes, ISWI, SWI/SNF, CHD and INO80. Biochemically, members of the different classes superficially show a similar ability to slide nucleosomes. Yet in vivo, these enzymes play non-redundant roles in transcription, DNA replication and repair. Why these motors have different biological roles remains an open question. A major area of research in the lab tackles this question by carrying out deep mechanistic analysis of chromatin remodelers from these different families. Our inquiries so far have led to the discovery of unexpected histone octamer plasticity (Sinha et al., Science 2017), auto-inhibition mechanisms that tightly regulate remodeler specificity for ISWI and INO80 enzymes (Leonard and Narlikar, Mol Cell, 2015; Gamarra et al., Elife 2018; Kaur & Wu et al., Science, 2025) and the preference by INO80 for subnucleosomal particles that are generated during transcription (Hsieh & Gourdet et al., Mol Cell 2022; Wu & Muñoz et al., Science 2023). Together these discoveries are shedding light on how the different biochemical consequences and regulation of chromatin remodelers explains their distinct biological roles.

An equally critical role in remodeling chromatin is played by abundant architectural proteins. Different architectural proteins have opposing roles on nucleosome dynamics, but how such opposing activities are coordinated is unclear. To better understand the underlying biochemical mechanisms, we are studying the interplay between linker histones (also known as H1) and abundant HMG box containing proteins such as HMGB1. Our findings suggest that HMGB1 counteracts the repressive effects of linker histones on nucleosome dynamics by co-occupying a nucleosome and displacing the interactions made by H1 C-terminal tail with nucleosomal DNA (Saunders et al., Science Advances, 2025). This new co-occupancy based model explains how the more weakly binding HMGB1 proteins can counteract the effects of the more strongly bound H1 proteins.

How do chromatin regulators operate in and influence crowded chromatin?

+

In the nucleus, any factor acting on a nucleosome must operate within a highly crowded chromatin environment. Estimated nucleosome concentrations in nuclei are ~100 μM or higher. These high concentrations coupled with the polymer nature of DNA is expected to generate a highly viscous environment. Such considerations raise the fundamental question of how any rapid chromatin biochemistry can occur in the nucleus. To address this question, we have reconstituted crowded chromatin in a test-tube using the intrinsic property of chromatin to compact into phase-separated condensates. Using this system we are studying how ATP-dependent chromatin remodelers and architectural proteins act in a crowded and viscous environment. At the same time we are also studying their mesoscale impact on chromatin. So far, we've found that remodelers from the ISWI class and SWI/SNF class can each remodel nucleosomes within crowded chromatin but have distinct meso-scale effects (Moore et al., Science 2025). ACF, a major ISWI remodeler associated with heterochromatin, spaces nucleosomes without de-condensing the chromatin, explaining how this complex maintains nucleosome organization in transcriptionally repressed genomic regions. In contrast, a major SWI/SNF complex, RSC catalyzes ATP-dependent de-condensation of chromatin while also driving micron-scale movements of entire condensates. The biological importance of remodelers may thus reflect both their effects on nucleosome mobilization and the corresponding consequences on chromatin dynamics at the mesoscale.

In terms of ATP-independent regulation, previous work by others has shown that H1 binding reduces chromatin dynamics and increases the viscosity of condensed chromatin. We've found that HMGB1 counteracts these effects of H1, making the chromatin more fluid (Saunders et al., Science Advances, 2025). Thus, a major role of proteins such as HMGB1 may be to globally regulate the material properties of chromatin to control the overall threshold for factor binding and chromatin biochemistry in general.

How does heterochromatin play so many different cellular roles?

+

A highly conserved type of heterochromatin is mediated by the interaction of HP1 proteins with chromatin that is methylated on Histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9me). While initially discovered through its repressive effects on transcription, this type of heterochromatin is also implicated in other cellular functions such as enabling proper chromosome segregation and maintaining nuclear rigidity. To understand how heterochromatin plays roles from the gene scale to the nucleus scale we have been reconstituting HP1-mediated heterochromatin and studying its biochemical and biophysical properties. To date, we have found that HP1 proteins can bridge nucleosomes promoting chromatin compaction through phase-separation based mechanisms (Canzio et al., Molecular Cell 2011; Larson et al., Nature 2017; Sanulli et al., Nature 2019). Such compaction appears to rely on distortion of the nucleosome core by HP1 proteins (Sanulli et al., Nature 2019). We have further discovered that HP1-DNA phases in a test-tube can recapitulate key properties attributed to heterochromatin in cells such as mechanical stability and long-lived DNA compartments (Keenen et al., Elife 2021). These properties rely on the specific structural features of HP1 proteins and the polymer properties of the DNA and chromatin, highlighting the need to study a given phase-separating chromatin system in depth to uncover its unique biologically relevant properties. Specifically we are continuing to study how the biochemical and biophysical properties of heterochromatin are regulated by HP1 paralogs, linker histones and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling.
